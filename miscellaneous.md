## Explain clearly | How would you explain something technical to a non-technical person?

. First, I would **profile the recipient**— what they already know, their role in the company, their level of technical experience, and whether they have worked on similar projects before.

From there, I would identify the **prerequisite knowledge** they need, pitch the explanation at the right level, and avoid introducing technical detail that does not help them understand the main idea.

I would then **scaffold the explanation** from something familiar, using a practical example, analogy or visual where useful. Finally, I would check their understanding and adjust the explanation if necessary.

**Recall cue:** Profile → prerequisites → level → scaffold → example → check.



Yes. This is a very good example for that **“explain something technical to a non-technical person”** interview question, because Erik can show that he changes both the **amount of technical detail** and the **language** depending on the audience.

I’d use **PCP — Personal Contract Plan**, rather than PPC.

For a clean interview example, assume an **Audi A6 costing €65,000**, a **3-year PCP**, an illustrative **6.9% APR**, and a final optional payment of **€29,250**. Actual PCP quotations would vary by dealer, APR, fees and guaranteed minimum future value.

# Explaining a PCP to Different Audiences

## The three customer scenarios


| Up-front payment | Approx. monthly payment | Final payment if keeping car |
| ---------------- | ----------------------- | ---------------------------- |
| **€0** | **€1,270/month** | **€29,250** |
| **€15,000** | **€808/month** | **€29,250** |
| **€32,500** | **€268/month** | **€29,250** |


The key trade-off is simple: **the more capital you put in at the beginning, the lower the monthly repayment, but the final optional payment remains substantial.**

## Audience 1 — Completely non-technical customer

I would avoid terms such as residual value, amortisation and discounted cash flow.

I might say:

> “The car costs €65,000. You don't necessarily have to pay for the whole car over the next three years. Part of the cost is left until the end.
>
> If you put nothing down today, your monthly payments are about €1,270.
>
> If you put €15,000 down, they fall to about €808.
>
> If you put half the price down, €32,500, they fall to about €268.
>
> At the end of the three years, you have a decision. You can return the car, subject to the agreement's conditions, or pay the remaining €29,250 and own it.”

The important thing here is that I am explaining the **decision structure**, not the mathematics.

**Recall cue:** Today → monthly → three years → hand back or buy.

## Audience 2 — Financially comfortable but non-specialist

Here I could introduce the idea of **deferred value**.

> “A PCP effectively splits the €65,000 price into three parts: your deposit, your monthly repayments, and a large amount deferred until the end.
>
> In this example, €29,250 is deferred for three years. That is why the monthly repayment can be much lower than on a conventional loan.
>
> Increasing your deposit from zero to €15,000 reduces the monthly payment from about €1,270 to €808. Putting €32,500 down reduces it further to about €268.
>
> But I would also point out that a lower monthly payment does not necessarily mean the car is cheaper overall. We need to look at the interest rate, total amount payable and final payment.”

That shows a little more financial understanding without overwhelming the customer.

**Recall cue:** Deposit → deferred value → monthly cost → total cost.

## Audience 3 — Financial or quantitative colleague

Now Erik can explain the mechanics.

> “The PCP is essentially a financed cash-flow structure with a terminal balloon payment.
>
> The vehicle price is €65,000 and the terminal value is assumed to be €29,250 after 36 months. The monthly payment therefore amortises only part of the financed amount while also covering interest on the outstanding balance.
>
> With zero deposit, approximately €65,000 is initially financed and the payment is about €1,270 per month. A €15,000 deposit reduces the financed principal and brings the payment to approximately €808. A €32,500 deposit reduces it further to about €268.
>
> Because the €29,250 balloon remains outstanding throughout the agreement, interest is still associated with that deferred capital. So I would compare the alternatives using total cash paid or present value rather than simply comparing monthly repayments.”

Now he is speaking the language of **cash flows, principal, terminal value and financing cost**.

## Why this is such a strong interview example

It demonstrates exactly what the question is testing.

The underlying financial product has not changed. **The explanation changes because the recipient changes.**

For the first customer:  
**“What do I pay now, every month, and at the end?”**

For the second:  
**“Why does the monthly payment change, and what does it cost overall?”**

For the technical colleague:  
**“How are the cash flows structured and financed?”**

That would strengthen the answer currently on the website considerably because it turns **“profile the recipient”** into a concrete example Erik could actually talk through at interview.
