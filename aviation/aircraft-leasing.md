## What do you know about aircraft leasing?

An airline can buy an aircraft or lease one from a **lessor** such as Abelo. Leasing gives the airline capacity without paying the full purchase price up front. The **lease agreement** sets the rent, term, maintenance and return obligations. For the lessor, the aircraft produces income, but its condition and future value still need to be protected. Modern aircraft leasing has a strong Irish connection through Tony Ryan and GPA in Shannon.

The part that interests me is looking at **both sides of the deal**. The airline pays lease rent to use the aircraft. The lessor may borrow to buy it and pays interest on that funding. There is a difference between rental income and financing cost, but that difference is **not pure profit**: the lessor also has to allow for maintenance exposure, administration, downtime between leases, credit risk and changes in the aircraft's value. Asset Management helps keep the rent, aircraft records and lease obligations on track.

## How long do the two sides last?

**Average remaining lease term** is the average time left on current customer leases. It indicates how far ahead contracted rent is visible. **Average remaining debt tenor** is the average time before the lessor's borrowing matures or needs to be refinanced. A long lease with debt maturing sooner can leave the lessor exposed to a new borrowing rate while the rent is still fixed.

In its *Aircraft Leasing 101* investor education transcript, AerCap said that **at the end of Q1 2024**, its average remaining lease length was **over seven years** and its average remaining debt tenor was **approximately six years**. Those are AerCap's dated portfolio averages, not a standard lease structure or figures for Abelo. Averages also hide the timing of individual aircraft and loans.

## Fixed rates, hedging and the funding spread

AerCap described its delivered leases as almost exclusively **fixed rate**, and its funding as predominantly fixed rate too. Some floating-rate debt can be **hedged** with interest-rate swaps or caps. Matching the timing and rate exposure of lease income with borrowing is part of **asset–liability management**. The **funding spread** is the difference between what the aircraft earns and the cost of financing it, before the other costs and risks above.

Imagine an aircraft on a fixed-rent lease while its borrowing rate rises each year. The airline keeps paying the agreed rent, but the lessor's interest bill may increase. Fixed-rate borrowing or an appropriate hedge can reduce that mismatch. When the lease ends, the lessor must also decide whether to extend it, **re-lease** the aircraft to another airline, or sell it; the aircraft's condition and **residual value** matter to that decision.
